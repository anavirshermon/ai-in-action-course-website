## Read this first

This guide answers one question for every assignment: what exactly am I supposed to submit, by when, and how do I know if it is any good.

It does not teach you how to build anything. That is the Handbook (`03-Student-Guide-Building-with-AI.md`). This document is only about deliverables.

The two sections carry different weights, different due dates, and in two cases different assignments. Pick your track at the top of the course website and these pages will show only what applies to you. If you have not picked one, you will see both.

Some words used throughout, defined once:

- **MVP** stands for minimum viable product. It means the smallest working version of your product that tests whether your idea is right. Not a bad version of the finished thing. The smallest thing that answers the question.
- **Repository**, usually shortened to **repo**, is your project folder stored on GitHub. When an assignment asks for a repo link, it wants the GitHub web address.
- **Deployed** means your app is on the internet at a real web address, not just running on your laptop.
- **Probe** means a small, deliberately disposable thing you build in weeks 4 and 5 to test whether anyone cares about a problem.
- **Build log** is your own weekly record of what you did. It is graded.

## How grading works

Twenty percent of your grade comes from class participation, which means contributing to discussion, engaging in the lab, giving other teams useful feedback, and showing up. In the undergraduate section, attendance is separately graded and worth another five percent. The rest comes from the assignments above.

Grades on the team assignments are adjusted by a **peer evaluation multiplier**. At the end of the semester everyone confidentially rates their teammates' contributions, and that produces a multiplier applied to your team grades. The graduate multiplier runs from 0.85 to 1.10, and the undergraduate multiplier from 0.95 to 1.05. If you carried the team, this helps you. If you did not, it does not. The multiplier does not touch your individual assignments.

## About the two build arcs

This course has you building things from week two onward. You are building two very different kinds of thing, and knowing which is which will save you some frustration.

**Weeks 4 and 5 are probes. You are meant to throw them away.**

You will build a landing page for each candidate problem area your team is considering. That is it. No database. No features. No weekend spent making it beautiful.

The purpose is not to build a product. The purpose is to find out whether anyone cares before you spend six weeks building. Many probes fail. That is a successful outcome rather than a failed one, and finding out in September usually costs you one evening instead of a semester.

Try not to get attached to what you build in weeks 4 and 5.

**Weeks 6 through 9 are the product. This one you keep.**

After your team commits to an idea, you build one real thing and improve it every week until demo day. You add two or three features at week 6, integrate real components such as a database or an LLM call at week 8, and iterate on design and testing at week 9.

Here the risk is the opposite one. Try not to abandon this because it is ugly in week 7. It is supposed to be ugly in week 7.

## First App and Build Memo

**Graduate: 10%, due 9/16. Undergraduate: 5%, due 9/15. You do this alone.**

### What this is

Any small working web app that you built with AI and put on the internet, plus a memo about building it. It does not have to be related to your team's venture, and it does not have to be impressive. A tip calculator counts.

### Why you are doing it

So that you, personally, know you can do this before your team forms.

Most teams have one person who is more comfortable with technology, and on many teams that person quietly ends up doing all the building. This assignment exists so that does not happen to you. By week four you will have shipped something with your own hands, and you will spend the rest of the semester knowing you can.

It is also graded generously on purpose. This is an on-ramp rather than a filter.

### What to submit and when

**Graduate.** Due **9/16**, before Session 4.

**Undergraduate.** Due **9/15**, before Session 4.

Submit three things:

1. A live web address where your app can be opened
2. A link to your GitHub repository
3. A memo explaining what you built and why: what market problem it solves, and how customers would use it

**Graduate.** Your memo is two pages single-spaced, and must also cover the challenges you faced along the way. What broke, how you recovered, and which AI-prompting techniques worked well for you.

**Undergraduate.** Your memo is one page single-spaced.

### What a strong version looks like

- The app does one thing and that one thing works when I click it
- The memo names specific failures. "I got an error saying the module was not found, and the fix was X"
- The memo shows you understood what you were doing, rather than that you got lucky
- **Weak version:** "I built a website. It was hard at first but then it worked." That tells me very little and scores near the bottom of the memo points.

## Product Proposal

**Both sections: 5%. Team assignment. Graduate due 9/30, undergraduate due 9/29.**

### What this is

A team-written proposal outlining your group's candidate ideas for a new product.

**Graduate.** Three pages single-spaced, covering three ideas.

**Undergraduate.** Two pages single-spaced, covering two ideas.

### Why you are doing it

It forces the conversation your team would otherwise put off. Committing an idea to paper, with a reason attached, makes it much easier to tell a real opportunity from one that only sounded good out loud.

It also gives me a chance to push back before you spend six weeks building the wrong thing.

### What to submit and when

**Graduate.** Due **9/30**, before the Assessing Competition and Moats session.

**Undergraduate.** Due **9/29**, before the Assessing Competition and Moats session.

One document per team, briefly covering, for each idea:

1. Why your group is interested in it
2. What problem it solves, and for whom
3. The novelty, value, and uniqueness of the proposed solution
4. Why AI can help you build it

More details will be available on Canvas.

### What a strong version looks like

- The problem is described from the customer's side, not from the solution's side
- The ideas are genuinely different from each other rather than variations on one idea
- You say something honest about which one you currently favor and why
- **Weak version:** three descriptions of software features with no named customer and no reason to think anyone wants them.

## Progress Week Meeting

**Undergraduate section only: 5%. Team assignment. In class 10/6.**

### What this is

A fifteen to twenty minute meeting with me during class time in Session 7, where your team walks through where you have got to on the Discovery Report.

### Why you are doing it

The Discovery Report is the largest team deliverable in the first half of the course, and the most common failure mode is a team that discovers a hole in its evidence a few days before it is due. This meeting is there to find the hole while there is still time to fix it.

### What to submit and when

Nothing is submitted through Canvas. Come to class on **10/6** with a rough plan of action for the Discovery Report: which sections you have drafted, what evidence you have collected so far, and which parts you know are still thin.

Your progress is graded as "Poor Progress," "Marginal Progress," "Good Progress," or "Excellent Progress," and each corresponds to a particular score.

### What a strong version looks like

- You can name specific evidence you have already gathered, including interviews you have already run
- You are candid about what is missing rather than presenting a polished front
- The whole team shows up and more than one person can speak to the work
- **Weak version:** a team that has divided the report into sections and not started any of them.

## Discovery Report

**Graduate: 20%, due 10/21. Undergraduate: 15%, due 10/20. Team assignment.**

### What this is

A team-written summary of the research your group conducted across the discovery sessions: finding market problems, understanding customers, assessing competition and moats, and working out a business model.

**Graduate.** Up to twelve double-spaced pages, plus figures, appendices, and references.

**Undergraduate.** Up to eight double-spaced pages, plus appendices and references.

Appendices do not count toward the page limit.

### Why you are doing it

This is the document that stops you from building something nobody wants. It is also the thing your pitch gets checked against in December, so the evidence in it needs to be real.

Writing it forces you to notice what you do not actually know yet. Many teams discover a hole in their argument while writing this, which is exactly when you want to discover it.

### What to submit and when

**Graduate.** Due **10/21**, with the Mapping Product Infrastructure session.

**Undergraduate.** Due **10/20**, with the Mapping Product Infrastructure session.

One report covering:

- The market problem you are solving, defined explicitly, and why now is the right time to target it
- The size of the target market, with the assumptions behind your estimate stated
- What you learned from customer research
- An analysis of your competitors
- Your defensibility argument: what makes your product's proposed moat hold up
- A business model with planned unit economics

You will use AI throughout to conduct this research, and you must verify and validate what it gives you. **AI is not permitted for the writing of the report.** AI-generated interviews are not evidence and will not be counted.

### What a strong version looks like

- Quotes are specific and sometimes inconvenient. A report where every quote supports your idea reads as cherry-picked, because it usually is
- What your landing-page probes actually showed is reported plainly, including the ones that did not work
- The strategy visibly follows from the evidence rather than sitting next to it
- Every market number has a source you could hand to someone else
- **Weak version:** interviews that all say "yeah that sounds useful," no probe data, and a strategy section that could have been written before you talked to anyone.

## MVP Demo

**Both sections: 10%. Team assignment. In class 11/4 (graduate) and 11/3 (undergraduate).**

### What this is

A live demonstration of your working product, in front of the class, on the projector, followed by questions from a peer panel and me.

**Graduate.** Eight minutes.

**Undergraduate.** Six minutes.

It is the product running live. It is not a slide deck about the product.

### Why you are doing it

Because a thing that works in front of an audience is the clearest proof that it works. Almost everything else can be described convincingly whether or not it is true.

The question period is the other half. It tends to reveal in about ninety seconds whether you understand what you built.

### What to submit and when

**Graduate.** The demo happens in class on **11/4**.

**Undergraduate.** The demo happens in class on **11/3**.

Submit through Canvas the same day:

- Your live product web address
- Your GitHub repository link

Grading covers whether the product runs, whether and how evidence presented in the Discovery Report visibly shaped the product and its features, the quality of your testing and validation, and your responses to questions during the presentation.

Start scheduling any user testing early. Other people's calendars are usually the slow part.

### What a strong version looks like

- The core workflow runs start to finish without you explaining around a broken bit
- You can say "we changed X because user Y did Z"
- You are honest about limits. "Our summarizer degrades past two pages so we cap it" scores better than claiming it always works
- You narrate what the user gets, rather than what technology you used
- **If the live demo breaks**, switch to your backup recording and keep going. Panicking usually costs more than the break itself.
- **Weak version:** three half-built features shown quickly, no evidence of testing, and answers to questions that do not match what is on screen.

## Individual Build Log

**Both sections: 15%. You do this alone. Graduate due 12/9, undergraduate due 12/8.**

### What this is

Your own weekly record of what you personally did on the project, kept from Session 4 onward.

**Graduate.** Entries run about three pages each.

**Undergraduate.** Entries run about two pages each.

This is yours. It is not shared with your team, and your teammates do not write it.

### Why you are doing it

This course cannot really grade your code, because AI wrote most of it. What it can grade is your judgment.

There is also a blunter reason. Most assignments after week four are graded as a team. Without this, a student could contribute nothing from October onward and still do fine. This is the assignment that shows what you personally did.

Do not leave it to December. It cannot be reconstructed.

### What to submit and when

**Weekly from Session 4.** Keep a file called `build-log.md` in your project. Each entry covers what you built that week, what unique things you tried, what broke along the way, how you fixed it including dead ends, and what you learned.

**Graduate.** Final submission due **12/9**. Combine your four most interesting weekly logs into a single document.

**Undergraduate.** Final submission due **12/8**. Combine your four most interesting weekly logs into a single document.

**Your final submission must also describe a case where AI was confidently wrong, how you caught it, and what you learned from the incident.** This is not optional and it carries the most weight in the assignment.

**Undergraduate.** Your combined build log is graded as "Poor," "Average," "Good," or "Excellent," and each corresponds to a particular score. More details are on Canvas.

### What a strong version looks like

The test is whether someone reading an entry could tell that you wrote it rather than any of your teammates.

- **Weak:** "Worked on the signup form. Had some issues but fixed them. Learned about databases."
- **Strong:** "Tried to add email signup. Claude built it and said it was working. The form submitted and showed a success message but no emails arrived in Supabase. Claude insisted the code was correct. I opened the Supabase dashboard directly and the table was empty. It had created the table under a different name than the code was writing to. Lesson: a success message in the browser proves nothing about the database."

The second one takes about four minutes to write and is worth several times the first.

## Final Venture Package and Pitch Presentation

**Both sections: 20%. Team assignment. Graduate: pitch 12/2, package 12/9. Undergraduate: pitch 12/1, package 12/8.**

### What this is

Two things graded together. A live pitch presentation, and a final package of documents submitted the following week. Both sections get five minutes of Q&A after the pitch.

**Graduate.** A ten-minute pitch, judged live by external judges.

**Undergraduate.** An eight-minute pitch.

### Why you are doing it

The demo proved the product works. This has to prove the business works, which is a different argument requiring different evidence.

### What to submit and when

**Graduate.** Pitch Day is **12/2**, in class. The Venture Package is due **12/9**.

**Undergraduate.** Pitch Day is **12/1**, in class. The Venture Package is due **12/8**.

The Venture Package contains:

- The final pitch deck, with an appendix of supporting evidence
- A live product web address and a GitHub repository link
- A two-page double-spaced **AI-usage report** documenting the most impactful uses of AI over the semester, such as pivotal prompts and agent workflows
- The **Discovery Report, revised and reframed as a Go-to-Market Strategy document**. This is an update rather than a rewrite, so every substantive change must be visibly annotated with highlighting or a short change log, making it clear what shifted and why
- **Real customer evidence**, meaning documented interviews with actual people and what you learned from each

**Graduate.** A minimum of six real interviews.

**Undergraduate.** A minimum of four real interviews.

More details will be provided in class and on Canvas.

### What a strong version looks like

- Your market size is built bottom up: number of customers times price, with a source for the customer count. **Never** "one percent of a fifty billion dollar market"
- You have prepared answers to the questions investors usually ask AI startups: what is your moat when the model improves, why does the incumbent not just do this, what do inference costs do to your margins, is this a feature or a company, and what lock-in accrues over time
- Your numbers match your Discovery Report and your demo. Anything appearing for the first time in the pitch will probably be asked about
- The change log in your Go-to-Market document shows real revision rather than reformatting
- When you do not know, you say "I don't know, and here is how we would find out." That is usually a good answer. Bluffing is graded down, and experienced judges tend to notice.

## The rules about AI

Using AI is the course. You are expected to use it constantly, for building, research, drafting, and critique, and your use of it is itself graded.

**1. Every factual claim in submitted assignments needs to be manually verified.** AI generates confident, plausible, and often fabricated citations and claims. You are graded on its errors exactly as if they were yours, because they are. Fabricated citations are referred as academic integrity matters.

**2. All interview and user-test evidence must come from real humans.** AI characters are useful for practice and are never data. Submitting synthetic responses as evidence is an integrity violation.

**3. Your AI-usage documentation must be truthful.** The build log and the AI-usage report are graded on insight, not on how little AI you used. There is no advantage in understating it, and there is a real cost to misrepresenting it.

**4. No AI-slop.** Writing generated by AI is often difficult to read, or convoluted in its logic and evidence. For written assignments, please plan and write without AI, and use AI only for revising and iterating on arguments and flow. This tends to improve the quality of your writing considerably. AI is not permitted for the writing of the Discovery Report at all.

**How this is enforced:** any team member can be asked any question about any part of a submission during the demo or the pitch. If you did not do the work, that is usually where it shows.
