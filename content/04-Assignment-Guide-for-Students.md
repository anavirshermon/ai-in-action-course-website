## What you have to hand in, when, and what good looks like

## Read this first

This guide answers one question for every assignment: what exactly am I supposed to submit, by when, and how do I know if it is any good.

It does not teach you how to build anything. That is the Handbook (`03-Student-Guide-Building-with-AI.md`). This document is only about deliverables.

Some words used throughout, defined once:

- **MVP** stands for minimum viable product. It means the smallest working version of your product that tests whether your idea is right. Not a bad version of the finished thing. The smallest thing that answers the question.
- **Repository**, usually shortened to **repo**, is your project folder stored on GitHub. When an assignment asks for a repo link, it wants the GitHub web address.
- **Deployed** means your app is on the internet at a real web address, not just running on your laptop.
- **Probe** means a small, deliberately disposable thing you build in weeks 4 and 5 to test whether anyone cares about a problem. More on this below.
- **Build log** is your own weekly record of what you did. It is graded. More on this below.

## How grading works

Your grade has two parts. **Eighty percent comes from the assignments above.** **Twenty percent comes from class participation**, which means contributing to discussion, engaging in the lab, giving other teams useful feedback, and showing up.

Of the eighty percent, **twenty-five percent you earn entirely on your own** and **fifty-five percent you earn with your team.**

The team portion is adjusted by a **peer evaluation multiplier**. At the end of the semester everyone confidentially rates their teammates' contributions. That produces a multiplier between 0.85 and 1.10 applied to your team grades. If you carried the team, this helps you. If you did not, it does not. Anyone rated below 70 percent gets a conversation with me before grades are posted. The multiplier does not touch your individual assignments.

## About the two build arcs

This course has you building things from week two onward. But you are building two very different kinds of thing, and knowing which is which will save you a lot of frustration.

**Weeks 4 and 5 are probes. You are meant to throw them away.**

You will build a landing page for each problem your team is considering, put an email signup form on it, and send real people to it. That is it. No database. No features. No weekend spent making it beautiful.

The purpose is not to build a product. The purpose is to find out whether anyone cares before you spend six weeks building. Most probes fail. That is a successful outcome, not a failed one, and finding out in September costs you one evening instead of a semester.

Do not get attached to what you build in weeks 4 and 5.

**Weeks 6 through 11 are the product. This one you keep.**

After your idea locks at the end of week 5, you build one real thing and improve it every week until demo day. Walking skeleton in week 6, first feature in week 7, and so on.

Here the risk is the opposite one. Do not abandon this because it is ugly in week 7. It is supposed to be ugly in week 7.

## First App and Build Memo

**Graduate: 5%. Undergraduate: 10%. You do this alone. Due 9/16.**

### What this is

Any small working web app that you built with Claude Code and put on the internet, plus a one-page memo about building it. It does not have to be related to your team's venture. It does not have to be impressive. A tip calculator counts.

### Why you are doing it

So that you, personally, know you can do this before your team forms.

Every team has one person who is more comfortable with technology, and on every team that person quietly ends up doing all the building. This assignment exists so that does not happen to you. By week four you will have shipped something with your own hands, and you will spend the rest of the semester knowing you can.

It is also graded generously on purpose. This is an on-ramp, not a filter.

### What to submit and when

Due **9/16**, before Session 4. Submit three things:

1. A live web address where your app can be opened
2. A link to your GitHub repository
3. A one-page memo covering: what you built, two things that went wrong and how you got past them, and one prompt that worked well and why you think it worked

### What a strong version looks like

- The app does one thing and that one thing works when I click it
- The memo names specific failures. "I got an error saying the module was not found, and the fix was X"
- The memo shows you understood what you were doing, not just that you got lucky
- **Weak version:** "I built a website. It was hard at first but then it worked." That tells me nothing and scores near the bottom of the memo points.

## Industry AI-Impact Memo

**Graduate: 10%. Not assigned in the undergraduate section. You do this alone. Due 9/30.**

### What this is

A 1,200 to 1,500 word analytical memo about one industry or one occupation, arguing where AI substitutes for human work, where it complements it, and what opening that creates for a new company.

### Why you are doing it

Two reasons.

First, it makes you take a position and defend it with evidence, which is a different skill from building. Your team project will not make you do this individually anywhere else.

Second, it feeds your venture directly. The "why now" argument in your Discovery Report is much stronger if you have already thought hard about what specifically changed in your industry and when.

### What to submit and when

Due **9/30**, before Session 6. One document, 1,200 to 1,500 words, covering four things:

1. Where will AI substitute for human labor in this industry?
2. Where will it complement human labor instead?
3. What opportunity does that create for a new entrant?
4. What evidence would prove your argument wrong?

You must cite at least five sources, at least one of which is an empirical study. The course reading list for this memo counts. Include a three-line note on how you used AI to write it.

### What a strong version looks like

- Question four is answered seriously. Most memos skip it or answer it weakly, and it is where the marks are
- Claims are attached to evidence you actually read, with page numbers or quotes
- You take a position that someone could disagree with
- **You are graded on your sources being real.** If AI invents a citation and you submit it, that is on you, and it is an academic integrity matter.

## Discovery Report

**Both sections: 15%. Team assignment. Due 10/14.**

There is no class on 10/14. This is a submission, not a class session. Your last discovery session is 10/7, which gives you a week to write.

### What this is

A report, twelve pages maximum for graduate teams and ten for undergraduate, that documents everything you learned about your problem before you committed to building for it. Appendices do not count toward the page limit.

### Why you are doing it

This is the document that stops you from building something nobody wants. It is also the thing your pitch gets checked against in December, so the evidence in it needs to be real.

Writing it forces you to notice what you do not actually know yet. Most teams discover a hole in their argument while writing this, which is exactly when you want to discover it.

### What to submit and when

Due **10/14**. One report covering:

- The problem and why it is solvable now specifically
- **Customer evidence.** At least **eight real interviews** for graduate teams, **five** for undergraduate. For each: who they were, when you spoke, and two direct quotes. *(Working alone: six for graduate, four for undergraduate.)*
- **Probe results.** What your landing pages from weeks 4 and 5 actually showed. Include the ones that failed.
- A competitor matrix where every claim has a source link
- Your defensibility argument, including a direct answer to "what happens if Anthropic ships this as a feature next quarter?"
- Strategy and positioning
- Business model with a simple unit economics table
- Your kill criteria: what would make you abandon or change this?

**AI-generated interviews are not evidence and will not be counted.** You may practice interviewing against an AI character. You may never submit that as data.

### What a strong version looks like

- Quotes are specific and sometimes inconvenient. A report where every quote supports your idea reads as cherry-picked, because it usually is
- Failed probes are reported plainly. **You get points for this.** Two of the fifteen points are specifically for what your probes showed including what did not work
- The strategy visibly follows from the evidence rather than sitting next to it
- Every market number has a link
- **Weak version:** eight interviews that all say "yeah that sounds useful," no probe data, and a strategy section that could have been written before you talked to anyone.

## MVP Demo

**Both sections: 20%. Team assignment. In class 11/4.**

### What this is

A live demonstration of your working product, in front of the class, on the projector. Eight minutes, followed by five minutes of questions from a peer panel and me.

It is the product running live. It is not a slide deck about the product.

### Why you are doing it

Because a thing that works in front of an audience is the only proof that it works. Everything else can be described convincingly whether or not it is true.

The question period is the other half. It reveals in about ninety seconds whether you understand what you built.

### What to submit and when

The demo happens **in class on 11/4**. Submit the same day:

- Your live product web address
- Your repository link
- Your edge-case testing report from the Session 9 exercise
- Summaries of at least **three user tests you ran with real humans**
- If your product uses AI inside it, your evaluation of whether that feature actually produces good output
- Your individual build log, for the checkpoint (see next assignment)

Start scheduling the user tests early. Other people's calendars are the slow part, and you have from 10/21 to 11/4. The tests are assigned at Session 8 and the protocol for running them is taught at Session 9 — send the scheduling messages before you know the protocol, not after.

### What a strong version looks like

- The core workflow runs start to finish without you explaining around a broken bit
- You can say "we changed X because user Y did Z." This is worth five of the fifteen points
- You are honest about limits. "Our summarizer degrades past two pages so we cap it" scores better than claiming it always works
- You narrate what the user gets, not what technology you used
- **If the live demo breaks**, switch to your backup recording and keep going. You lose one point. Panicking costs more.
- **Weak version:** three half-built features shown quickly, no evidence slide, and answers to questions that do not match what is on screen.

## Individual Build Log

**Graduate: 10%. Undergraduate: 15%. You do this alone. Checkpoint 11/4, final 12/11.**

### What this is

Your own weekly record of what you personally did on the project. One entry a week, about five minutes, starting in week 4 and running to the end. Roughly ten entries.

This is yours. It is not shared with your team, and your teammates do not write it.

### Why you are doing it

This course cannot grade your code, because AI wrote most of it. What it can grade is your judgment.

There is also a blunter reason. Every other assignment after week four is graded as a team. Without this, a student could contribute nothing technical from October onward and still do fine. This is the assignment that shows what you personally did.

For undergraduates it is worth fifteen percent, which is the largest individual piece of your grade. Do not leave it to December. It cannot be reconstructed.

### What to submit and when

**Weekly from 9/16.** Keep a file called `build-log.md` in your project. Each entry: what I tried, what broke, how I fixed it including dead ends, what I learned, what is next.

**Checkpoint, 11/4.** Submit the log as it stands with your team's demo. Graded on whether it is current, specific, and honest.

**Final pass, 12/11.** Mark your best entries and write a short annotation on each: what you were trying to do, what AI gave you, what you changed. **Graduate section: four entries. Undergraduate section: three.**

**One of your annotations must describe a time AI was confidently wrong, you caught it, and what it would have cost you if you had not.** This is not optional and it is the most heavily weighted single thing in the assignment.

### What a strong version looks like

The test is whether someone reading an entry could tell that you wrote it rather than any of your teammates.

- **Weak:** "Worked on the signup form. Had some issues but fixed them. Learned about databases."
- **Strong:** "Tried to add email signup. Claude built it and said it was working. The form submitted and showed a success message but no emails arrived in Supabase. Claude insisted the code was correct. I opened the Supabase dashboard directly and the table was empty. It had created the table under a different name than the code was writing to. Lesson: a success message in the browser proves nothing about the database."

The second one takes four minutes to write and is worth several times the first.

## Venture Package and Pitch Day

**Both sections: 20%. Team assignment. Pitch 12/2, package due 12/11.**

### What this is

Two things graded together. An eight-minute investor pitch delivered live on 12/2 to external judges and me, with four minutes of questions after it. And a final package of documents submitted on 12/11.

### Why you are doing it

The demo proved the product works. This has to prove the business works, which is a different argument requiring different evidence.

The judges are real outside investors and founders. Their questions are the ones you would actually get.

### What to submit and when

**Pitch Day, 12/2, in class.** Eight minutes, then four minutes of questions. *(Undergraduate: three minutes of questions.)* Graduate teams present a ten-slide deck, undergraduate teams eight slides.

**Venture Package, due 12/11:**

- Final deck as a PDF
- Live product web address and repository link
- Evidence appendix: your updated interview and user test log, probe results, and any usage data
- **Prompt book:** five to ten of the prompts or agent workflows that mattered most, with commentary on what worked

**The prompt book and your build log are not the same document.** The prompt book is your team saying "here is how our venture got built." The build log is you saying "here is what I personally did." Both are due 12/11.

### What a strong version looks like

- Your market size is built bottom up: number of customers times price, with a source for the customer count. **Never** "one percent of a fifty billion dollar market"
- You have prepared answers to the five questions investors always ask AI startups: what is your moat when the model improves, why does the incumbent not just do this, what do inference costs do to your margins, is this a feature or a company, and what lock-in accrues over time
- Your numbers match your Discovery Report and your demo. Anything appearing for the first time in the pitch will be asked about
- When you do not know, you say "I don't know, and here is how we would find out." That is a good answer. Bluffing is graded down and experienced judges always notice.

## The rules about AI

Using AI is the course. You are expected to use it constantly, for building, research, drafting, and critique, and your use of it is itself graded.

Three rules have no exceptions.

**1. All interview and user-test evidence must come from real humans.** AI characters are useful for practice and are never data. Submitting synthetic responses as evidence is an integrity violation.

**2. Every factual claim in graded work needs a checkable source.** AI produces confident, plausible, occasionally invented citations. **You are graded on its errors exactly as if they were yours, because they are.** Fabricated citations are referred as academic integrity matters.

**3. Your AI documentation must be truthful.** The build log and prompt book are graded on insight, not on how little AI you used. There is no advantage in understating it and there is a real cost to misrepresenting it.

**How this is enforced:** any team member can be asked any question about any part of a submission during the demo or the pitch. If you did not do the work, that is where it shows.
